Understanding AI Models

How LLMs work in practice, and why they make the mistakes they do.

Tokens: The Smallest Unit

Tokens are pieces of text. One token roughly equals 4 characters or 0.75 words. Models break everything—prompts and responses—into tokens before processing.

Why it matters: Tokens are how AI tools measure usage and cost. "Hello" might be 1 token, but "Hello_world" could be 2. Every prompt and response consumes tokens from your context window.

Pro tip: Don't worry about keeping prompts short—context is king. The more relevant detail you give, the better the output. Modern context windows are large enough that you should focus on clarity, not brevity.

Context Window: Your Conversation Limit

The context window is the total number of tokens a model can see at once. Modern models have 1M+ token windows, but there's a catch: effective capacity is usually 60–70% of the advertised max. A model claiming 200K tokens works reliably around 130K.

Example: If your context window is 128K tokens and your prompt is 100K tokens, you only have 28K for the model's response.

For development work, a 128K+ window is standard. You can paste entire codebases, logs, and documentation in one prompt.

Hallucinations: When Models Confabulate

Hallucinations are confident-sounding but false outputs. Models don't "know" they're wrong—they predict the next token based on patterns, not facts.

Why they happen:

  • Training data had errors or conflicting info
  • You asked about something outside their training or knowledge
  • Ambiguous prompts that the model tried to fill in

How to minimize: Give specific, factual context. Ask the model to cite sources. For critical facts, verify independently.

Why Models Make Mistakes

  • Knowledge cutoff: Models were trained on data up to a certain date, but most modern tools now use web search to fill in the gaps. This is less of an issue than it used to be.
  • Tokenization artifacts: Sometimes token boundaries create weird biases. A model might finish sentences oddly because of how tokens split.
  • Lack of reasoning: Larger models with extended thinking can reason step-by-step, but basic models just predict the next token. No introspection.
  • Context confusion: If you paste conflicting information, the model gets confused about which version is correct.
  • Training bias: If training data was skewed, the model's outputs will reflect that skew.

The Practical Bottom Line

Models are fast, powerful pattern-matchers. They're not search engines, databases, or guarantees. For development:

  • Use them to brainstorm, debug, and explain concepts
  • Don't trust hallucinations without verification
  • Give them context—more info = better outputs
  • Iterate—your first prompt rarely gets the best answer
  • Know your tool's limitations—most now search the web, but always verify critical info

The best use case: "Here's my code and error. Why is it breaking?" The worst: "Is [random fact] true?"
